Chain-of-Thought Safety and Mechanistic Interpretability

Research note by Chunlin Gong · Updated October 28, 2026

This research guide is written by a coauthor of CoT is Not the Chain of Truth (ICML 2026).

Chain-of-thought (CoT) safety concerns the safety of content generated during a reasoning model's deliberation. A model can produce unsafe reasoning and subsequently refuse in its final answer. This guide connects that evaluation problem to mechanistic interpretability: identifying safety-related layers and attention heads, testing their behavioral role, and evaluating targeted interventions. CoT is Not the Chain of Truth provides an empirical case study using fake-news generation and HarmBench.

How should we evaluate chain-of-thought safety?

A useful evaluation records the prompt, the generated reasoning trace, and the final response as separate observations. Trace labels describe the content generated during reasoning. Final-response labels describe the answer presented after that reasoning. Joint evaluation reveals cases in which unsafe trace content precedes a safe final response, including a refusal.

CoT is Not the Chain of Truth applies this approach to fake-news generation. Its safety taxonomy distinguishes three reported categories, shown below. The interpretation of each statistic depends on the evaluated model, prompt setting, and category. These definitions provide a concrete protocol for researchers studying process-level safety or comparing refusal behavior with trace content.

Which layers and attention heads are associated with unsafe CoT?

Mechanistic interpretability connects an observed behavior with the computations of a model. In this study, layer-level analysis localizes depth intervals where safe and unsafe generations differ. Attention-head analysis then characterizes routing behavior within those intervals, giving researchers specific components to examine.

For a softmax routing vector p, the analysis uses the Jacobian J = diag(p) - pp^T. B1 is its spectral norm and describes local routing sensitivity. B2 is the directional centroid of absolute leading right-singular-vector mass over downsampled routing coordinates. B3 is the entropy effective rank of the normalized squared singular values and describes how broadly spectral participation is distributed.

Perturbation experiments test how changes to the identified components affect behavior. This provides a workflow from safety labels to layer localization, head-level measurements, and behavioral tests. Researchers interested in attention-head mechanisms, refusal behavior, or targeted model control can use the workflow as a starting point for their own evaluation settings.

How do CoT safety, refusal, and mechanistic interpretability connect?

Research on reasoning models asks several connected questions: what content a trace contains, how refusal decisions develop, which internal components influence behavior, and how a verbalized trace relates to the computation producing an answer. How does trace safety relate to CoT faithfulness and monitorability?

Trace safety concerns the safety of the generated reasoning text. CoT faithfulness concerns the relationship between a verbalized explanation and the computation producing an answer. Monitorability concerns the information that a monitor can recover from observations of model behavior. These questions motivate different annotations, interventions, and evaluation protocols.

The contribution described here is the empirical analysis of generated trace safety and associated internal model components. The fake-news and HarmBench settings specify the evaluated tasks; the layer/head analysis and targeted updates specify the mechanism and intervention evidence. This description helps readers select the study for a related-work discussion or an empirical comparison.
